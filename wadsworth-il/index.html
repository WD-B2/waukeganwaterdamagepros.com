When water is spreading in Wadsworth

If water is moving through a house in Wadsworth, the first priority is to stop it and make the space safe. Do these things while dispatch arranges a crew.

  • Close the main water valve if the source is a pipe. If your home runs on a private well, shut off the well pump at its breaker only if the area around the panel is dry.
  • Keep out of rooms where water has reached outlets or the panel, and call the utility or 911 for any electrical or gas concern before you call us.
  • Lift valuables, rugs and electronics off the floor, and keep children and pets away from standing water.
  • Photograph the waterline and the damaged rooms before moving anything.

The water problem to plan for here: clay, a river and slow drainage

The village sits on the east bank of the Des Plaines River, according to the Chicago Historical Society encyclopedia. That puts river flooding on the list of questions for any Wadsworth homeowner, and the FEMA Flood Map Service Center is the place to see whether your address is mapped. We do not state flood zones for individual streets.

Clay is the second clue. By 1886 two drainage tile factories and a brick factory stood near the railroad station, because the area had abundant clay. Clay soil drains slowly, so water can sit near a foundation after rain. If a basement or crawl space in Wadsworth stays damp, ask a drainage contractor about the soil around your house, since cleanup does not stop seepage.

When water does get in from outside, flood damage cleanup explains how contaminated water is handled, and basement water damage cleanup covers a flooded lower level.

A village built on a rail line and a milk run

The Chicago, Milwaukee and St. Paul Railroad finished its line through here in 1873. A farmer named John Lux platted the village in 1874, and the railroad named the stop for Elisha Wadsworth, a major stockholder. The station mainly collected milk from nearby dairy farms for Chicago.

The practical point is that Wadsworth grew from farmland, not from a planned subdivision grid. Houses of different ages sit on lots of different sizes, and many properties are larger than a typical city lot. Tell dispatch whether your property has a private well, a septic system or a long driveway culvert, because each changes what the crew checks first.
